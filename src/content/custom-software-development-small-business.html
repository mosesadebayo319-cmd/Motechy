Custom software is worth considering when an important business workflow cannot be handled well by a suitable existing tool. Define the problem, test off-the-shelf options and estimate the cost of the current process before commissioning a build. The first release should support a complete, useful workflow with clear acceptance criteria.

A small organisation may begin with a spreadsheet, a shared folder and messages between colleagues. That can be entirely sensible. The difficulty starts when nobody can tell which version is current, who approved a change or whether the information in a report matches the underlying records.

Software can help, but “we need an app” is too broad a brief. The business may need a better configured existing service, a small integration, a customer portal or a custom application. This guide explains how to choose, what a development project should include and how to prepare requirements a developer can estimate.

Describe the workflow before naming features

Choose one recurring task and follow it from the trigger to the final outcome. For example: a staff member records an activity, attaches supporting evidence, submits it for review, and a manager uses approved records in a report. Write down who does each step, where information is stored and what happens when something is missing.

That description is more useful than a shopping list of “dashboard, AI, notifications and analytics.” It reveals the actual rules. Who can approve? Can an author approve their own submission? Can a final report change? What should happen if a file upload fails halfway through?

Record the present cost in terms the business understands: staff time, repeated data entry, missed handovers, delays and corrections. Use observed figures where available. If you only have an estimate, label it and record the assumptions rather than presenting it as a measured saving.

Illustrative starting calculation: three staff members each spend four hours per week reconciling records. At an assumed internal cost of ₦5,000 per hour, that task represents ₦60,000 per week. This is a planning estimate, not money a new application automatically saves. Some review work will remain, and implementation, training and support have costs too.

Compare buying, configuring and building

First test whether a suitable existing product handles the core task. Use a short trial with realistic sample data and the people who will do the work. A feature list may look convincing while the permission model, export format or mobile experience does not fit your requirements.

Choose an approach around the workflow
Approach Useful when Check carefully
Use an existing product Your process broadly matches a common task such as scheduling or invoicing User fees, permissions, export options, support and required integrations
Configure or connect tools The main tools work, but information is copied manually between them API limits, failed-sync handling, duplicate records and ownership of the connection
Build custom software The workflow or rules are important and existing options leave material gaps Delivery scope, maintenance, operational ownership and the total cost over time

Ask each supplier to demonstrate your highest-risk step. If separate organisations must not see each other’s records, test that separation. If the system must produce a particular report, inspect an actual export. If you need to leave the product later, test the data export before signing a long agreement.

Custom development can offer a closer fit, but it transfers more decisions to the business and its developer. Someone must own the roadmap, support arrangements, hosting costs and future changes. Buying a subscription does not remove those responsibilities entirely; it changes how they are shared.

Do you need a website, a portal or an application?

A public website mainly communicates information and supports actions such as enquiries or purchases. A portal usually gives a defined group access to their information or tasks. A business application manages records and workflows, often with several roles and rules.

The distinction changes the work. A company contact page may require a validated form and dependable delivery. A customer portal may additionally need authentication, access control, account recovery, data retention decisions and a way to handle incidents. Both need testing, but the risk and operational scope differ.

Write a brief that can be estimated

You do not need to prescribe a programming language. You do need to make the business requirements visible. A useful first brief includes:

  1. Problem and goal: the task that needs improvement and how you will recognise a useful result.
  2. Users and roles: who can view, create, edit, approve and administer information.
  3. Workflow: the normal path, exceptions and decisions at each stage.
  4. Data: the records, files, existing sources and information that needs protection.
  5. Integrations: systems the application must read from or write to, and whether access is available.
  6. Devices and conditions: phones, desktops, expected connectivity and any genuine offline requirement.
  7. Constraints: available budget, target date, approval process and dependencies.
  8. Handover: who will operate the system and what training, access and documentation they need.

Attach anonymised sample forms, report layouts and spreadsheet columns where useful. Avoid sending live customer records merely to illustrate a requirement. A small representative example usually communicates the structure without exposing personal information.

Define a first release that completes a real task

A minimum viable product should let someone complete the core job. A collection of attractive screens with disconnected actions does not establish that. Choose a narrow workflow and take it through input, validation, storage, review where needed, and a useful output.

For a booking tool, the first release might support choosing an available slot, submitting required details, receiving a confirmation and cancelling under agreed rules. Advanced recommendations or a loyalty scheme can wait. Failure handling for a double booking cannot wait if appointments are the central promise.

Separate requirements into essential for launch, useful next and explicitly deferred. Record why each essential item is needed. This makes scope discussions more productive than describing every request as urgent.

Agree how changes are handled. A newly discovered requirement may be valid, but it can affect cost, timing and testing. Ask the developer to explain those effects before the work is added. Keep a visible list so important decisions are not scattered through message threads.

Turn requirements into acceptance criteria

“The system must be secure and easy to use” is an intention. Acceptance criteria describe observable behaviour. They help both the business and the developer know what must work before a milestone is complete.

  • A staff member can save a draft without submitting it for approval.
  • A reviewer can return a submission with a reason that its author can read.
  • A user in one organisation cannot retrieve another organisation’s records by changing a link.
  • A failed save preserves the entered information and explains how to retry.
  • A report export includes only records meeting the agreed status and date rules.
  • A backup can be restored into an isolated test environment and checked.

These are example criteria, not a specification for every application. Adapt them to your workflow, especially the exceptions. The OWASP Application Security Verification Standard provides a structured reference for technical security requirements. Ask your developer which controls apply and how they will be verified, rather than treating a framework name as proof of security.

What determines software development cost and timing?

The number of user roles, complexity of business rules, quality of existing data, external integrations, reporting requirements and operational needs all affect the work. A small interface can hide complicated rules. A larger set of straightforward screens may be easier to estimate.

Include discovery, design, implementation, testing, data migration, deployment and handover in the discussion. If an integration depends on another provider’s approval or documentation, record that dependency. Do not build a launch promise around access nobody has confirmed.

Compare the total cost over an agreed period. Include the initial project, hosting, database and storage, third-party services, maintenance, training, support and expected changes. For an existing product, include seat fees, configuration, integrations and exit costs. Use the same timeframe for both options.

A proposal can divide the project into milestones: confirmed requirements, reviewed prototype, a tested core workflow, and a release with handover. Define the acceptance evidence for each. The right cadence depends on scope; a standard number of weeks cannot honestly cover every custom application.

Agree ownership and support before launch

The contract should explain access to source code, data, hosting and third-party accounts, including any licences or components that cannot be transferred. Specify who pays recurring bills and who can administer the system. A business should know how it would continue operating if its developer became unavailable.

Plan the operational work too. Who receives error alerts? Who handles a failed payment or an email delivery problem? What is backed up, how often, and how is restoration checked? What response can you expect outside normal hours? These answers affect both service quality and support cost.

Training should cover the real tasks staff perform, including correcting mistakes and escalating problems. Start a pilot with a manageable group, collect specific feedback and fix workflow problems before a wider rollout. A successful deployment is the beginning of operation, not evidence that adoption has already happened.

Questions before commissioning software

Should every business build its own software?

No. A suitable existing product may be faster to adopt and easier to support. Custom development should address a meaningful gap, with a clear owner and a defensible budget.

Should the application include AI?

Only when it solves a defined task and the results can be evaluated. Consider accuracy, review, privacy, provider costs and a fallback. AI is not a substitute for clear business rules or reliable storage.

Can we build in stages?

Yes. Keep the first release narrow enough to test properly, but complete enough to do useful work. Agree what later phases may require so an early shortcut does not quietly become a permanent constraint.
